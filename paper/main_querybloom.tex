\begin{abstract}
Text-SPARQL example sets are indispensable for training, tuning, and evaluating Knowledge Graph Question Answering (KGQA) systems, yet they remain scarce for various knowledge graphs due to the extent of manual effort required. We present \textit{QueryBloom}, an automated, KG-agnostic methodology that synthesizes diverse, execution-verified Text-SPARQL examples requiring only a SPARQL endpoint. The approach systematically samples structurally diverse multi-hop subgraphs from the knowledge graph, then employs an LLM to verbalize each subgraph into natural language questions and corresponding SPARQL queries, with execution-based feedback used to iteratively verify and correct each generated pair. Evaluation against the human-authored DBpedia QALD-9 benchmark demonstrates that QueryBloom matches or exceeds established benchmarks in query structural complexity, semantic linguistic variety, and schema coverage.
\end{abstract}

Constructing high-quality Text-SPARQL datasets is a costly and time-consuming process, requiring experts who are proficient in formal SPARQL query construction, fluent in the target domain, and thoroughly familiar with the semantics, structure, and vocabulary of the knowledge graph as captured by its ontology. Existing curated benchmarks concentrate on a handful of prominent open-domain knowledge graphs, principally DBpedia, Wikidata, and Freebase, and rely on manual question authoring and contributions from the open-source community \cite{usbeck2019qald,usbeck2021qald,trivedi2017lc,dubey2019lcquad,wikisp2023,liu2024spinach}.\todo[color=green]{Make it clear that QALD and so on are such existing benchmarks on open-domain KGs.}\todo[color=green]{Unclear why we discuss each benchmark's creation}. Domain-specific knowledge graphs, which encode specialized knowledge in fields such as biomedicine \cite{kgs2025biomedical}, geoscience \cite{sockg2025}, and cultural heritage, lack equivalent community infrastructure and consequently have few or no Text-SPARQL resources.

The resulting scarcity has shaped the trajectory of the field with the majority of KGQA research \cite{liu2024spinach,zahera2024generating,sgpt2022generative,kacupaj2023sparqlgen,banerjee2022modern,diallo2025frase,sun2022think,wikisp2023} operating exclusively over open-domain graphs where datasets already exist, leaving domain-specific settings largely unaddressed. Meanwhile, existing approaches, ranging from prompt-based methods \cite{zahera2024generating,emonet2024llm} and fine-tuned translation models \cite{sgpt2022generative,neural2022machine} to agentic systems \cite{liu2024spinach,walter2025grasp}, could in principle be generalized to other knowledge graphs if Text-SPARQL examples were available. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has addressed the automatic generation of reliable, diverse, and comprehensive Text-SPARQL pairs for arbitrary knowledge graphs. The most relevant concurrent work, KGQAGen~\cite{zhang2025kgqagen}, is limited to Wikidata and is designed to address factual quality issues in existing benchmarks, which is a fundamentally different objective.

\textbf{Text-SPARQL Datasets.} Several curated benchmarks have driven progress in KGQA. The QALD challenge series~\cite{usbeck2019qald,usbeck2021qald} provides hand-crafted questions over DBpedia and Wikidata, while LC-QuAD~\cite{trivedi2017lc} and LC-QuAD 2.0~\cite{dubey2019lcquad} use template-based generation with human paraphrasing to scale dataset size. WikiSP~\cite{wikisp2023} sources questions from search logs to better reflect real user information needs. Spinach~\cite{liu2024spinach} curates questions from Wikidata's ``Request a Query'' forum, capturing authentic complexity beyond synthetic benchmarks. All of these resources target open-domain knowledge graphs (Wikidata, DBpedia, Freebase); domain-specific graphs, which lack equivalent breadth or community efforts, have no comparable Text-SPARQL coverage. \todo[color=green]{It seems obvious that they cannot be transferred.}

\textbf{KGQA via NL-SPARQL Translation.} KGQA has seen significant advances with the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs), which have achieved state-of-the-art performance compared to prior deep learning and classical approaches \cite{dabramo-etal-2025-investigating,sgpt2022generative}. LLM-based approaches include chain-of-thought prompting for SPARQL generation \cite{zahera2024generating}, fine-tuning on text-SPARQL pairs \cite{sgpt2022generative,neural2022machine}, and in-context learning with retrieved examples \cite{emonet2024llm}. Agentic approaches such as GRASP~\cite{walter2025grasp} and Spinach~\cite{liu2024spinach} employ LLMs with tool-use for interactive graph exploration.\todo[color=green]{you mentioned these are not fully accurate.} All of these approache require or at least benefit from Text-SPARQL examples. QueryBloom is thus, complementary to these systems, as it can automatically provide the example sets they require for arbitrary knowledge graphs.

\textbf{Synthetic Data Generation.} Template-based approaches such as LC-QuAD~\cite{trivedi2017lc} generate questions from fixed SPARQL templates, but require manual template design per knowledge graph. Labate et al.~\cite{labate2025synthetic} recently proposed using LLMs to replace crowdsourced paraphrasing of LC-QuAD's template-generated questions, showing that synthetic rephrasings can match human quality for fine-tuning; however, their method still depends on LC-QuAD's existing template infrastructure and is limited to Wikidata. The most closely related concurrent work, KGQAGen~\cite{zhang2025kgqagen}, employs LLM-guided iterative subgraph expansion over Wikidata with SPARQL-based verification to construct high-quality question–answer pairs. However, it is tethered to Wikidata, seeds entity selection from Wikipedia, and uses SPARQL primarily as a factual consistency oracle rather than as the target output to improve factual quality issues observed in earlier benchmarks. Moreover, it emphasizes popular entities such as notable people, events, in the generation process. QueryBloom, in contrast, is different in two fundamental aspects: (i) it is designed to be KG-agnostic framework for bootsrapping KGQA datasets over arbitrary RDFS knowledge graphs, and (ii) it is designed to generate diverse and comprehensive KGQA example sets that cover a wide range of query patterns and knowledge graph semantics as opposed to prioritizing popular entities specifically. Unlike most RDF knowledge graphs, Wikidata employs a statement-based data model that does not conform to W3C RDFS semantics and therefore falls outside QueryBloom's scope.


\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The central observation underlying QueryBloom is that every SPARQL query implicitly specifies a subgraph pattern over the knowledge graph. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:sparql_subgraph}, the triple patterns in a \texttt{WHERE} clause define nodes and labeled edges that must be matched in the graph, while \texttt{SELECT} projects specific variables, and \texttt{FILTER} imposes value constraints. Each returned result row corresponds to a distinct embedding of this pattern in the graph data: a concrete assignment of entities to the query's variables that satisfies all structural and value constraints. The same argument applies to other SPARQL query forms (\texttt{ASK}, \texttt{CONSTRUCT}), which rely on the same \texttt{WHERE} clause pattern matching mechanism.

QueryBloom inverts this correspondence: it first discovers structurally diverse subgraph patterns from the knowledge graph and then jointly synthesizes SPARQL queries and natural language questions grounded in those patterns. 

The pipeline, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:querybloom_pipeline}, proceeds through the multiple stages described below, which trace how a single subgraph pattern is discovered, grounded, and distilled into a small set of text-SPARQL pairs. The framework relies on the minimal assumption that the target knowledge graph follows W3C RDFS standards for defining classes, which is a common practice in almost all RDF knowledge graphs.

\subsection{Pattern Discovery and Adjacency Indexing}

The pipeline begins by extracting \textit{frequent type patterns} from a
property scope chosen for the target knowledge graph, adapting the ABSTAT
algorithm~\cite{Spahiu2016ABSTATOL} for efficient pattern extraction over RDF
graphs. A type pattern is a triple $(\textit{SubjectType},\;\textit{Predicate},
\;\textit{ObjectType})$ encoding that a given property connects instances of one
type to instances of another (e.g., \texttt{Person} --\texttt{worksAt} --\texttt{Organization}); patterns are discovered by sampling a large set of
triples for each property and resolving the \texttt{rdf:type} of their subject
and object nodes.

QueryBloom supports two preprocessing modes that differ in how the property
scope is determined. \textit{Whole-graph mode}, suited to smaller knowledge
graphs, enumerates all properties via a SPARQL query, removes common metadata
predicates such as labels and identifiers, and extracts patterns from the
resulting set. \textit{Online-graph mode}, suited to large graphs, begins from a
single user-specified seed property, extracts its type patterns, and then
iteratively selects properties connected to already-discovered types, adding
their patterns until a configurable property budget is reached.

In both modes, the collected patterns are filtered by a minimum support
threshold $\sigma$; the resulting set of patterns form the structural building blocks for
subgraph construction. An adjacency index is then built over this pool, mapping
each type to every pattern in which it participates and enabling constant-time
lookup of candidate extensions during subgraph discovery.

\subsection{Subgraph Discovery}

A multi-hop subgraph pattern is grown from a single frequent pattern, which serves as a two-type seed. The seed is iteratively extended by attaching adjacent patterns from the adjacency index that share at least one type with the current subgraph. Each extension adds a new edge and may introduce new types, continuing until a subgraph with $k$ distinct types is formed. At each extension, the subgraph's support is verified via a SPARQL \texttt{COUNT} query and the extension is discarded if it causes the subgraph's support to fall below the threshold $\sigma$. It follows that the subgraph will contain at least $k-1$ properties and may be cyclic or acyclic.

Once a k-type subgraph is formed, it is enriched in two further steps. First, data attributes are sampled from the discovered attribute patterns and attached to the subgraph's types and verified. Second, $m$ concrete entity instances per type that jointly satisfy the subgraph's type and property constraints are fetched from the endpoint, along with their attribute values. The subgraph comprises the abstract subgraph pattern and grounding instances with real entity URIs, labels, and attribute values to inform realistic question generation.


\subsection{LLM-Driven Text-SPARQL Distillation}

Each verified subgraph is processed by a four-stage multi-agent system whose collective purpose is to distill the subgraph into a small set of high-quality, execution-verified text-SPARQL pairs. The four agents — a \textit{generator}, a \textit{complexity introducer}, a \textit{verifier}, and a \textit{reword auditor} — operate in sequence, each receiving the subgraph as a structured YAML representation. All agents use structured output with enforced JSON schemas to guarantee perfectly parseable responses \cite{openai2024structured}.

\paragraph{Generation.}
The generator agent first assesses whether the subgraph pattern admits meaningful questions about the knowledge graph, exiting early if not. If meaningful, it generates a set of text-SPARQL pairs grounded in the subgraph's classes, entities, relationships, and attributes (see Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_prompt}). Each generated SPARQL query is immediately executed against the endpoint, and its results are recorded alongside the pair.

\paragraph{Complexity Introduction.}
To ensure that generated question–query pairs match the complexity of real-world examples, a
\textit{complexity introducer} agent rewrites each successfully executed pair to meaningfully incorporate
additional non-trivial constructs drawn from a predefined set: value filtering, optional matching,
set union, sorting and limiting, existential checks, nested subqueries, and recursive relationships. Both the SPARQL query and its corresponding natural language question are rewritten
consistently to reflect the introduced construct. Where no meaningful augmentation is possible,
the agent skips the pair and retains the original. Every augmented query is re-executed against
the endpoint to update the execution results.

\paragraph{Verification.}
Although grounding generation in a concrete subgraph pattern is in principle sufficient to produce accurate text-SPARQL pairs (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), an LLM is still prone to hallucination or innovation while forming the text-SPARQL. An iterative verifier agent therefore inspects every pair. Given the subgraph pattern and each pair's question, query, and execution results, the verifier judges each pair as \textit{correct} or \textit{needs-fix}. For pairs it deems in need of fixing, it additionally produces corrected question and query texts; the corrected queries are re-executed and the updated pairs are fed back into the next verification round. The loop runs for up to a configurable number of iterations or until all pairs are marked correct.

\paragraph{Question Rewording.}
As a final step, a reword agent audits the natural language question of each correct pair for KG-specific terminology leakage — such as exposed URI fragments, ontology class names, or other non-natural phrasings — and rewrites only the question text of flagged pairs, leaving the SPARQL query unchanged.

All four LLM calls operate with high reasoning effort to maximise correctness, and only pairs that pass verification are retained in the final dataset.

\subsection{Design Principles}

The pipeline is designed to produce a text-SPARQL example set that is \textit{comprehensive}, \textit{diverse}, and of \textit{high quality}.

\paragraph{Comprehensiveness.} The overall algorithm orchestrates the stages described above by seeding at least one subgraph from every frequent type pattern in the constructed pool. Thus, coverage is defined with respect to the chosen preprocessing mode: whole-graph mode covers all frequent patterns found among the graph's selected non-metadata properties, whereas online graph mode covers all frequent patterns found for the seed property and any connected properties added within the property budget. As opposed to graph mining, we deliberately adopt a very low support threshold because even rare subgraph patterns can give rise to meaningful questions.


\paragraph{Diversity.} Several mechanisms ensure diversity in the generated examples: (i) unlike a random walk that follows a linear path, the subgraph is extended by selecting a new pattern from \textit{any type} contained in the current subgraph, producing diverse structural compositions; (ii) the target number of types $k$ is sampled from a range distribution to produce subgraphs of varying sizes; (iii) for each subgraph, we randomly sample $v$ attributes per class to ensure diverse coverage of class attributes; (iv) the distiller agent is prompted to produce variations in question and query style, covering different query patterns (e.g., \texttt{SELECT}, aggregation, \texttt{ASK}, \texttt{FILTER}, \texttt{OPTIONAL}, \texttt{GROUP BY}).

\paragraph{Quality.} Quality is safeguarded at multiple stages. Subgraphs that are trivial or concern only graph metadata are flagged as non-meaningful during generation and excluded. The generate-execute-verify loop ensures that every retained pair produces results expected by the distiller agent. The distiller agent's core objective is to elicit questions from the subgraph that a domain expert would be expected to ask.

\textbf{Query Structural Complexity.} Figure~\ref{fig:querybloom_results_panels}(b) illustrates the distribution of SPARQL 1.1 operators within both datasets. QueryBloom demonstrates significantly higher structural complexity in several key dimensions compared to the QALD-9 baseline. Notably, QueryBloom queries incorporate \texttt{FILTER} constraints at roughly four times the rate of the baseline. Furthermore, they heavily utilize \texttt{OPTIONAL} blocks and property paths, which are entirely absent from the QALD-9 test set. Both datasets maintain a comparable usage of \texttt{UNION} operators. This confirms that QueryBloom maintains a higher level of structural complexity than the QALD-9 baseline which more closely reflects the complexity of real-world SPARQL queries.

\textbf{Semantic Linguistic Diversity.} To evaluate the natural language variety of the generated questions, we compute dense semantic embeddings for each question text using SBERT (BGE-large) and project these embeddings into a 2D space via t-SNE (Figure~\ref{fig:querybloom_results_panels}(a)). We compute Average Pairwise Distance (APD) for global diversity and Mean Nearest-Neighbour Distance (MNND) for local density. QueryBloom achieves a higher APD and MNND, which is visibly confirmed in Figure~\ref{fig:querybloom_results_panels}(a) by the broader, more evenly distributed spatial spread of its data points. This indicates that QueryBloom successfully covers a wider semantic space of linguistic intents and vocabulary than the fixed human-authored QALD-9 test set.

\textbf{Holistic Schema Coverage.} Figure~\ref{fig:querybloom_results_panels}(c) presents a multi-dimensional summary of dataset diversity across five key metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unique Entities \& Relations:} The average number of distinct entities and properties referenced per query, reflecting how broadly the dataset explores the underlying knowledge graph schema.
    \item \textbf{Variables \& Triples:} The median number of variables and triple patterns per query, measuring the structural size and depth of the generated SPARQL.
    \item \textbf{Unique Templates:} The ratio of distinct SPARQL skeletons (where specific entities, variables, and literals are anonymized) to the total number of queries, assessing syntactic novelty.
\end{itemize}

QueryBloom outperforms QALD-9 across almost all these dimensions. Most strikingly, it achieves a substantially higher unique template ratio, indicating that its SPARQL skeletons are even more diverse than those found in the human-authored benchmark. The next clearest difference is structural depth, with QueryBloom producing a median of roughly three times as many triple patterns per query as the baseline. Furthermore, it consistently incorporates a higher rate of unique entities per query, ensuring broader and more diverse coverage of the graph's instance data.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented QueryBloom, a KG-agnostic methodology for automatically synthesizing diverse, high-quality Text-SPARQL example sets from arbitrary RDF knowledge graphs, requiring only SPARQL endpoint access. Through multi-hop subgraph sampling and LLM-driven verbalization with execution-based verification, QueryBloom produces example sets that match or exceed the diversity of established human-authored benchmarks across query structural complexity, semantic linguistic variety, and schema coverage. By addressing the example scarcity challenge for domain-specific knowledge graphs, QueryBloom can support the development and evaluation of KGQA systems in specialized domains where curated resources are unavailable, while also serving as executable documentation that helps practitioners understand unfamiliar graph structures.
